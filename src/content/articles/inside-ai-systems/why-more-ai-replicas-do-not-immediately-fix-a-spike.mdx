## The practical problem

The student assistant receives a burst. Its autoscaler changes the desired replica count from two to six. The dashboard appears reassuring, yet first-token latency keeps rising.

The missing distinction is **desired versus ready**. A requested replica may be waiting for placement, downloading model artifacts, loading weights, initializing runtime state, or checking dependencies. Until those steps finish, it cannot help the waiting learners.

This lesson shows a proposed capacity-control path. Kubernetes is a documented example of the mechanisms, but the diagram is not a complete cluster configuration.

## Capacity is a lifecycle

For teaching purposes, track four states: requested, provisioned, starting, and ready. In a real platform these states may be represented differently, and transitions may fail.

A replica means an instance of the serving workload. It does not necessarily mean one independent accelerator: model parallelism and deployment configuration can change the hardware mapping. Likewise, replica counts alone do not establish available throughput.

Measure the transition times and failure reasons. A gap between desired and ready replicas tells you that capacity is not catching up; it does not tell you whether the cause is scheduling, startup, quota, artifact access, or another dependency.

## Follow the control path

<InsideAiArchitecture day={5} />

Load measurements inform the controller. The controller chooses a target under configured bounds. The platform provisions or places the workload. Startup prepares the model. A readiness gate determines whether the router may send live work to that instance.

The serving system closes the feedback loop by measuring the work accepted and completed by the ready pool. A bounded queue and overload response remain necessary while capacity is pending.

[Kubernetes describes the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler as an intermittent control loop](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/autoscaling/horizontal-pod-autoscale/). It can use resource or other configured metrics. Selecting queue age, concurrency, or another serving signal also requires collecting that metric and making it available through the chosen infrastructure; a model deployment does not supply that integration automatically.

## Work through the startup delay

Use an intentionally simplified example. The ready pool can complete 8 requests per second. A burst offers 20. The controller notices immediately, but useful additional capacity takes 15 seconds to arrive.

1. The shortfall is 12 requests per second.
2. During startup, a fluid approximation adds 12 × 15 = **180 requests** of backlog.
3. If newly ready capacity raises the aggregate rate to 24 and arrivals remain at 20, spare capacity is only 4.
4. Draining that backlog then takes about **45 seconds**.

These are invented rates with identical work, no cancellations, an unlimited queue, and fixed capacity. Real systems should cap accepted work; the calculation explains the pressure rather than recommending that backlog.

Adding just enough capacity to match arrivals prevents further growth but does not drain existing backlog. You need spare capacity, lower demand, expiry, rejection, or some combination.

## Choose a signal that reflects the problem

Average processor utilization can be useful, but it may not reflect model-serving pressure. For this teaching workload, compare queue age, admitted concurrency, input/output lengths, ready capacity, and successful throughput.

A queue-depth signal alone can also mislead. A queue of ten long-context requests may represent more work than a queue of fifty small ones. Calibrate the signal with representative request classes and observe whether scaling improves the user's latency objective.

Maintain a maximum replica count to bound resource use, and define behavior at that ceiling. A controller cannot create unlimited hardware merely because demand remains high. Check quotas and placement constraints before relying on a target.

## Startup, readiness, and liveness are different questions

**Startup** asks whether initialization has completed. **Readiness** asks whether the instance should receive traffic. **Liveness** asks whether a container is unhealthy in a way that warrants restart.

[Kubernetes documents these probe responsibilities and how a startup probe delays liveness and readiness checks until it succeeds](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/probes/). Probe behavior should match the service's actual lifecycle.

For this assistant, a port being open is too weak a readiness condition if model loading continues afterward. Define a check that establishes the serving dependencies required for a request. Keep the check inexpensive and avoid using aggressive restarts as an overload policy.

A failing shared dependency may make many replicas unready at once. Decide whether partial service is possible and which dependency failures justify removing an instance from routing.

## Warm capacity and scale-down choices

If a webinar is scheduled, prepare capacity before it ends. A warm minimum reduces dependence on startup during predictable demand, but consumes resources while idle.

For unpredictable demand, compare earlier scaling with a conservative ready minimum. Avoid cycling capacity up and down on every small fluctuation. A stabilization policy trades slower scale-down for less churn; its values depend on the workload and the infrastructure.

Scale-down must also account for active requests. Stop assigning new work to a draining worker, let appropriate work finish within a budget, and cancel or transfer work according to a documented contract. Removing a replica count is not the whole shutdown process.

## Common mistakes and limitations

Counting starting replicas as serving capacity understates overload. Scaling only after a large queue forms can produce help too late for interactive deadlines.

Making liveness checks too aggressive during initialization can repeatedly restart a model that would otherwise finish loading. Readiness without meaningful checks can route traffic prematurely.

A capacity model calibrated on short prompts may fail on longer contexts or outputs. Test the workload mix. This article does not provide production probe thresholds, autoscaler settings, or a guarantee that every hardware platform can provision additional resources within a chosen time.

## Exercise and completion checkpoint

Draw a timeline with four events: demand increase, controller decision, resources assigned, and readiness passed. Annotate the queue age at each point.

Repeat the fluid example with capacity 10, arrivals 16, and startup 8 seconds. The backlog is **48 requests**. If capacity then becomes 20 while arrivals stay 16, the simplified drain time is **12 seconds**.

The completion checkpoint is the arithmetic, a readiness contract, one scale-down rule, and a clear response when maximum capacity is reached. Identify the assumptions that need measurement before replacing the toy rates with a production plan.
