## A search problem before a language problem

A reader asks for “ways to reduce inference expense.” Your document is titled “Lowering model serving costs.” An exact word match may miss a useful result.

Before a system can compare a query and a document, it needs a representation of each. Different representations preserve different information.

## Start with counts

A bag-of-words representation records terms and their frequencies. It can be sparse and interpretable: one dimension corresponds to one vocabulary term. But word order is mostly lost.

TF-IDF combines term frequency with [inverse document frequency](https://nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book/html/htmledition/inverse-document-frequency-1.html), reducing the weight of terms that occur in many documents. The Stanford [information retrieval text](https://nlp.stanford.edu/IR-book/html/htmledition/term-frequency-and-weighting-1.html) explains term weighting. These lexical methods remain useful when exact identifiers, names, or specialist terms matter.

## Dense vectors change the comparison

An embedding maps an input into a vector. The training objective influences which inputs end up close under a chosen similarity measure. Related phrases may then match even without sharing the same words.

That similarity is not human understanding. An embedding can confuse related but contradictory statements, miss a critical number, or behave poorly in a domain unlike its training data.

Document retrieval adds further choices: whether to represent a sentence, paragraph, section, or entire document; how to preserve headings; and what metadata to retain. A vector detached from its source is difficult to inspect or cite.

## Evaluate with questions from the actual task

Create a small set of realistic queries and label relevant documents. Include paraphrases, abbreviations, rare identifiers, and queries for which the collection has no answer.

Compare lexical retrieval, vector retrieval, and a hybrid approach. Do not infer quality from a few satisfying demos. Examine missing results as well as high-ranked irrelevant ones.

## Try the contrast

Write two short documents that use different words to express the same idea. Then write two that share many words but disagree. Predict how a term-based and an embedding-based retriever might rank each.
